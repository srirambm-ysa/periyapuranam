Chapter 01 — The Hunchback King

In the seventh century the Pandyan land was ruled from Madurai by King Nedumaran. Because his back was hunched, the people called him Koon Pandiyan, the hunchbacked Pandyan. Some historians identify him with the Pandyan king Arikesari Parankusa Maravarman.

In those days Jainism had grown powerful in the south. Heeding the Jain monks, the king walked in their way; as Sekkizhar sings, he took the bewildering path for holy penance and was caught in the snare of the Jains who mortify their bodies. Shaivism began to decline in the land. Yet the same verse closes by saying his glory would one day spread across all seven worlds.

Madurai was the ancient capital of the Pandyas, and the Meenakshi Sundareswarar temple was the very soul of the city. Jain monasteries dotted the hills around Madurai, and with royal patronage their faith flourished while Shiva's temple worship languished.

Chapter 02 — The Queen and the Minister

The king's queen, Mangayarkkarasiyar, was a daughter of the Chola king — Sekkizhar hails her as "Valavar piran thirumagal," the blessed daughter of the Chola lord. She was an ardent devotee of Lord Shiva. His prime minister, Kulachchiraiyar, was likewise a steadfast servant of Shiva.

Seeing the Jain ascendancy swelling in the palace, the queen and the minister grieved and sought a way to redeem Shaivism. Just then they heard of the boy-saint Gnanasambandar of Seerkazhi — of Pugali — and the miracles flowering around him, and trusted that through the grace of the holy Child, the south would be won back.

The Child's fame had already spread across the Tamil land — he who sang "Thodudaya Seviyan" at the age of three in Seerkazhi. To the queen, herself born in the Chola country, his glory was well known; conferring with the minister, they resolved that bringing him to Madurai was the one true remedy.

Chapter 03 — The Invitation

At the queen's bidding, Minister Kulachchiraiyar invited Gnanasambandar by letter to grace Madurai. The Child accepted and came with his devotees, lodging in a hospice outside the city.

Learning of his arrival, the Jains took fright; fearing their influence would perish, they plotted to destroy him. One night they set fire to the hospice where he stayed, and the flames leapt high.

The queen and the minister received the Child quietly, lodging him in the hospice away from the eyes of the palace Jains. But the news spread, and the Jains, seized with dread, set the fire in the dead of night.

Chapter 04 — Fire in the Hospice

Seeing the hospice ablaze, Sambandar knew it for Jain work. He sang beginning "Payyave senru Pandiyarkkahave" — let it go softly and settle upon the Pandyan — turning the heat of the flames to fall slowly upon the king.

The next morning a cruel burning fever broke out upon the king's body — boils across his frame — and tormented him. Jain medicines and mantras failed; when they stroked him with peacock feathers, the burning only grew worse and brought no cure.

When the fever seized the king, the palace physicians panicked. The Jain monks chanted and stroked him with peacock feathers, yet the sickness only deepened, and the king sank to his bed. The queen and the minister judged the hour ripe and summoned Sambandar to the palace.

Chapter 05 — Healing with Sacred Ash

Hearing of it, Sambandar came to the king. Taking the holy vibhuti, he smeared it upon the king's body and sang the hymn beginning "Mandiramavadhu Neeru" — the mantra is the sacred ash. Before the song ended, the fever left him, and with it his hunched back straightened and stood erect.

All marvelled to see the king standing straight and tall, and glorified Lord Shiva. From that day he was hailed as Ninra Seer Nedumaran — Nedumaran who stood erect — and as Sundara Pandyan, the handsome Pandyan. His mind cleared, the king forsook Jainism and returned to Shaivism; as Sekkizhar says, through the grace of the holy Child, Shaivism overflowed through the southern land and the righteous sceptre guarded dharma.

The "Mandiramavadhu Neeru" hymn is chanted to this day across the Shaivite world as the mantra that cures disease. Seeing the hunchbacked king stand straight, the Jains stood confounded while the crowds roared Shiva's name. The king fell at the Child's feet and embraced Shaivism.

Chapter 06 — Fire and Water

The Jains called Sambandar's cure magic and demanded ordeals, the loser to be impaled — a condition both sides accepted. First the Anal Vadham, ordeal by fire: of the palm-leaves cast into the flames, Sambandar's Tirunallaru hymn-leaf came out green and fresh, while the Jains' leaf was reduced to ash.

Then the Punal Vadham, ordeal by water: cast into the Vaigai river, Sambandar's "Vazhga Andhanar" leaf floated upstream against the current and reached Tiruvedagam, while the Jains' leaf sank. Sambandar won both ordeals; as tradition tells it, the defeated Jains mounted the stake according to their vow, and Madurai breathed the fragrance of Shaivism again.

With both ordeals lost, Jainism fell in Madurai, and multitudes returned to Shaivism. Tiruvedagam, where the upstream-floating leaf came ashore, stands to this day as witness to Sambandar's victory.

Chapter 07 — The Battle of Nelveli

Restored to Shaivism, the king honoured Shiva's temples across the land and ruled with a sceptre that never swerved from dharma. When northern kings invaded, he marched out to meet them, and a fierce battle raged on the field of Nelveli.

Sekkizhar sings this battle across five verses — charging seas of cavalry, ranks of rutting elephants, sword-clash and drum-thunder roaring like the clouds of doomsday, ghosts and ghouls feasting upon the flood of blood, until the northern kings' hosts broke; wreathed in victory's Vagai with the Vembu, Nedumaran triumphed. It is this victory that Sundarar hails in the Thiruthondathogai: "Nelveli venra Ninra Seer Nedumaran."

After the Nelveli victory the king strengthened the Chola-Pandya bond, endowed temples with gifts, and ruled without swerving from justice. Sundarar's epithet "venra at Nelveli" fixed his fame forever in the Thiruthondathogai.

Chapter 08 — Among the Sixty-Three

Winning the war and guarding the country, the king then reigned long; he ruled so that the path of the sacred ash shone across the sea-girt world, and by the Lord's grace reached Sivaloka, says Sekkizhar. The Meenakshi Sundareswarar temple of Madurai shone again with Shaivite light.

Nedumaran's change of heart and lifelong service to Shaivism earned him a place among the Nayanmars — as it did for Queen Mangayarkkarasiyar and Minister Kulachchiraiyar beside him; Sekkizhar glorifies all three. Closing this puranam, Sekkizhar worships the feet of the Child of Pugali and turns to tell the holy service of Vayilar of Mylai. It is cherished to this day as the story of darkness lifting and Shaivism returning.

Mangayarkkarasiyar and Kulachchiraiyar are each honoured by name in the Thiruthondathogai. All three are honoured with guru-puja festivals in Shiva temples to this day.
